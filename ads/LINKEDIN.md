## 1. Lincoln & Euclid · `CTC_short_01_lincoln_euclid_LinkedIn_4x5.mp4`

Abraham Lincoln studied Euclid. Why don't our students?

As a young lawyer, Lincoln worked through Euclid's Elements, a 2,300-year-old geometry book, to learn what it truly means to prove something. For centuries, Euclid was part of a serious education.

Mathematics is not only about its applications. It teaches us how to think, and that matters even more now that AI can do the computing.

What schools can do:
→ Teach every topic with its history: who discovered it, and why it mattered.
→ Put primary sources, from Euclid to Euler, alongside practice that builds mastery.
→ Put mastery before speed, so no student moves on with a gap.

Three things any educator can start this week:
1. Ask students to explain *why* a method works, not just how.
2. Open one lesson with the story behind the idea.
3. Find one missing building block and go back to it before moving on.

## 2. Math is one story · `CTC_short_06_math_is_one_story_LinkedIn_4x5.mp4`

Math isn't a pile of worksheets. It's one story.

Many students experience math as disconnected units: a chapter, a test, then the next chapter. No wonder so many decide they're "not math people."

The fix is to teach math as one connected journey, from a child's first counting to calculus, where each idea grows out of the one before it. Students who can see the path keep walking it.

For educators:
→ Begin each unit by showing where it sits on the path, and what it unlocks next.
→ Revisit earlier ideas on purpose, so the story stays whole.
→ Celebrate the connections, not just the answers.

## 3. Where did the wheel come from? · `CTC_short_07_where_the_wheel_came_from_LinkedIn_4x5.mp4`

Where did the wheel come from?

Try moving something with a square tire. The wheel is one of the most important inventions in history, and it rests on the geometry of a circle.

When we open geometry with a question like this, students stop asking "when will I ever use this?" They see shapes, measurement and design in everything around them.

A simple PD idea: have teachers open their next geometry lesson with one everyday object and the history behind its shape. Ask for students' guesses first, then do the math.

## 4. Euler and Benjamin Franklin · `CTC_short_08_euler_and_franklin_LinkedIn_4x5.mp4`

Leonhard Euler and Benjamin Franklin lived at the same time.

Euler (1707–1783) was expected to become a pastor. His tutor, Johann Bernoulli, saw his gift for mathematics and encouraged his father to let him pursue it. Euler became one of the most productive mathematicians in history.

Two lessons for education:
→ A mentor who notices talent can change a life. Every student deserves a coach, not just a grader.
→ Placing mathematicians alongside the history students already know (Franklin, the American Revolution) makes math feel human and connected.

## 5. The people behind the math · `CTC_short_09_people_behind_ideas_LinkedIn_4x5.mp4`

Who were the people behind the math we teach?

We say "Newton's laws" and "Euler's identity" every day in classrooms, yet students rarely meet Newton or Euler as people. I recently stood in Mainz, Germany, where Gutenberg's printing press changed how knowledge spreads.

When students learn the context that gave rise to an idea, they understand it more deeply and remember it longer. They also see that ideas are made by people, which means they can make them too.

For educators: pick one name in your next unit and spend five minutes on the person behind it.

## 6. Who decided a minute has 60 seconds? · `CTC_short_10_sixty_seconds_LinkedIn_4x5.mp4`

Who decided a minute has 60 seconds?

The answer goes back thousands of years to the ancient Babylonians, who counted in base 60. Every time we read a clock, we use their mathematics.

Questions like this turn a routine lesson into an investigation. Students research where our clocks and calendars came from, and connect math to history, language and culture.

Try it as a cross-curricular project: "How did we get our clock and calendar?" Math, history and English teachers can share it.
