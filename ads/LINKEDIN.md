Abraham Lincoln studied Euclid. Why don't our students?

As a young lawyer, Lincoln worked through Euclid's Elements, a 2,300-year-old geometry book, to learn what it truly means to prove something. For centuries, Euclid was part of a serious education.

Mathematics is not only about its applications. It teaches us how to think, and that matters even more now that AI can do the computing.

Here is what we're doing about it at Counting to Calculus:
→ Every topic is taught with its history: who discovered it, and why it mattered.
→ Students read the greats, from Euclid to Euler, alongside the practice that builds mastery.
→ Mastery comes before speed, so no student moves on with a gap.
→ One connected path, from a child's first counting to calculus and the mathematics behind AI.

Three things any parent or educator can start this week:
1. Ask a student to explain *why* a method works, not just how to do it.
2. Share the story behind one idea (where zero came from, or who Pythagoras was).
3. Find one missing building block and go back to it before moving on.

If you're a parent of a student in grades 3–12, or an educator who wants to bring this approach to your school, I'd love to connect.
